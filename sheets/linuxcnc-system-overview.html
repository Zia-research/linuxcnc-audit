LinuxCNC — the whole system on one page

From the operator's screen down to the machine. The dashed red line is the only domain marker — no containers, because a frame asserts membership and can be contradicted by its own geometry, while a line cannot. The two shared-memory segments are drawn across it, which is what shared memory is. It began as a merge of two earlier sheets — Sheet B of the errata sheet for component detail, the context sheet's rebuilt view for the seams — and has been corrected seven times since, each correction and its evidence recorded at the foot of the page.

Sources: sheets/linuxcnc-code-notes-errata.html (Sheet B, the corrected block diagram — milltask internals, the emcmot segment's three structures, TC_QUEUE, the joint pipeline, component and driver inventories, the boundary wording) and sheets/linuxcnc-context-diagram.html ("Rebuilt from the code" — the linuxcncsvr / milltask process split, the three UI-side clients separated by what they touch, motion as functs inside the servo thread, the base-thread condition, the config column). Both verified against the pinned clone caa13ca6ae. Assembled 2026-08-06.

Restructured 2026-08-07, after three defects found by reading the geometry rather than looking at the figure. (1) The two domain containers claimed a membership their geometry did not hold: the frame labelled "ordinary Linux processes" ended at y=240, while linuxcncsvr (246) and milltask (300) sat below it — the same defect a reader had already caught in the sibling colour figure, fixed there and never here. (2) The emcmot segment was drawn wholly above the boundary, saying it belongs to ordinary scheduling. (3) HAL shared memory was not drawn at all, only captioned inside the realtime frame, which asserted it belongs to the realtime domain. Both segment claims are false: each is created on one side and attached from the other — motion.c:827 → usrmotintf.cc:578, and hal_lib.c:285 → halcmd.c:107, an ordinary process. The fix removes both domain frames. A dashed line marks the boundary and nothing else, the two shared segments are drawn across it, and the two connectors that used to land on a frame edge — from halcmd and from the .hal files — now land on HAL shared memory, which was always their real target. A line cannot make defect (1): there is no container left to contradict. (4) A fourth defect, found by a reader questioning the layout rather than the drawing: linuxcncsvr sat in a vertical chain between the NML triplet and milltask, which asserts that commands transit through it. They do not. In configs/common/linuxcnc.nml every process is LOCAL — emc, emcsvr and xemc alike — and only emcsvr carries server?=1 master?=1; task attaches the SHMEM segments directly, read-write. linuxcncrsh has no entry at all and opens its own channels. linuxcncsvr's two real jobs are creating the segments and starting first (scripts/linuxcnc.in:795) and serving them over TCP 5005 (emcsvr.cc:185 calls run_nml_servers(); its man page: "Allows network access to LinuxCNC internals via NML") — and its clients are remote, so none of them appears on this figure. The figure also contradicted itself: it already ran the GUI and linuxcncrsh straight to the triplet while routing task around. It is now drawn beside the triplet, and the triplet connects directly to milltask. (5) The same question asked once more exposed a label that was wrong twice: linuxcncrsh was captioned “NML over the network”. It is neither. On the network side it speaks a plain-text telnet protocol on its own port — emcrsh.cc:187 sets port = 5007, and :373-390 call socket(), bind() and listen(): it is itself the server, not a client of one. Its own header calls it an “Extended telnet based LinuxCNC interface”, and its man page “a text-mode interface for commanding LinuxCNC over the network… using telnet(1), nc(1) or similar”. On the LinuxCNC side it is an ordinary local NML client: emcrsh.cc:41 includes shcom.hh, which opens the channels as "xemc" — LOCAL in the default config — so it does write the triplet directly. It has no relationship with linuxcncsvr whatever: two independent servers, TCP 5007 text and TCP 5005 NML. The box is relabelled and both network doors are now drawn, because a door to the network that is not shown is a door nobody thinks about. Both now sit on the top edge, so that where the network touches this machine is visible at a glance; the canvas was widened and halcmd / halshow moved right to free the lane its own descent needs. Which door a client uses decides what it must be, and the difference is larger than it looks. The NML door is the narrow one. It carries no port of libnml — src/libnml/ has no Windows or Apple build — and no NML library is packaged apart from LinuxCNC, so “a remote NML client” means in practice another Linux running LinuxCNC as a GUI-only control PC. Even then it is not free: axis.py:75-78 gates AXIS's HAL component on the AXIS_NO_HAL environment variable, and HAL is local shared memory, so a remote AXIS must set it and give up its jog pins, pyvcp panels and classicladder detection. The shipped instructions have also rotted: client.nml still says to run tcl/tkemc.tcl, a GUI no longer present in the tree — only its screenshots survive in the documentation. The telnet door is the wide one: a socket and some text, so Windows, macOS, a tablet or a three-line script all qualify. Anything resembling MES, SCADA or a phone app belongs there, not on the NML side. A vocabulary warning, because it inverts easily: NML calls the machine the master — emcsvr carries master?=1 in server.nml — so describing the remote GUI as the “master PC” contradicts the very file you would then open. The two doors also differ in posture: linuxcncsvr authenticates nothing at all, while linuxcncrsh has a connection and an enable password (emcrsh.cc:189, default "EMCTOO") that its man page warns twice are “sent in the clear”. Red marks what is wrong rather than what is. Two problems on the NML door — it authenticates nothing, and its two config files look unmaintained — and one under the triplet, where the number itself lives: client.nml and server.nml declare emcStatus at 10240 B against the default's 20480, while NML gives a message only half the declared buffer (cms.cc:729-731). That last one is printed unverified on the figure itself, because sizeof(EMC_STAT) was never measured — a diagram that hides its own uncertainty is worse than one that admits it. The first words of the note scope it to the networked configs, so no reader concludes the default is affected. Also moved: “not NML on the network side” left the linuxcncrsh box for the grey peer above it. The phrase never described the box — it described the wire, and the grey box is what sits at the other end of it. (6) The GUI's link to HAL was drawn one-way. Reader's catch, and the source is blunt: halui.cc creates 47 HAL_IN pins against 42 HAL_OUT (:494, :531), and even axisui has inputs — axis.py:3961-3964 exports notifications-clear and resume-inhibit as HAL_IN alongside its twelve HAL_OUT jog pins. A GUI panel with a button writes a pin; the same panel's lamp reads one. Now double-headed. The remaining six one-way arrows were audited rather than left alone, and all six survive: the wizards generate the INI and HAL files, task reads the INI without writing it, a .hal file is loaded into HAL memory, and the two short arrows into tpmod / homemod / *kins are calls — the box says “called from motmod's functs”, which is one-way by construction. (7) The networked configs are not merely stale — they are not installed. src/Makefile:745-746 copies only linuxcnc.nml and linuxcnc_big.nml into $(prefix)/share/linuxcnc. A user installing from a package never receives client.nml or server.nml; they exist for whoever reads the source tree, which is why nothing exercised them and nothing caught the drift. The red note on the NML door says so, because “unmaintained” understates it. And the telnet door now carries its own red line: it does have two passwords, but its man page warns twice that they are sent in the clear, so marking only 5005 as unauthenticated would have let 5007 read as safe. Defects (4) and (5) share a lesson worth more than either: both were found by a reader questioning what the drawing asserted, and neither was reachable by any checker here — diagramCheck measures boxes, it does not read labels. Checked, not assumed: diagramCheck() reports clean — 19 boxes, 79 texts, 15 connectors. The straddle is tested directly rather than by rule 6, which needs two domain frames and there are deliberately none. The boundary was computed to cross only the two straddling boxes. Each of those three checks was first made to fail on a broken version, and the boundary is drawn as <line> rather than <path> so the connector check does not report the intended crossings as collisions — a checker that cries wolf on every run stops being read.
